\begin{abstract}
Genomic language models score sequence changes without task-specific training, but the step that turns a model score into an interpretation is a software contract, and software contracts fail in ways that models do not. This paper evaluates Evo2Serve, a web application that combines genome browsing, ClinVar lookup and contextual variant scoring with Evo 2-7B, along the axes a deployment must satisfy. We formalize the scoring contract as implemented, then measure it. On the 500-variant BRCA1 slice used for the deployed threshold, we reproduce the class composition of the source saturation-genome-editing assay (72.46\% functional, 21.14\% non-functional, 6.40\% intermediate, $n=3{,}893$) and show that the slice is class-enriched 7.79-fold relative to uniform sampling, so it supports only a narrow inference. We compute the first reproducible reference envelope for that slice: CADD reaches an AUROC of 0.791 (95\% bootstrap CI 0.750--0.835), aGVD 0.878 (0.821--0.929), phyloP 0.777 (0.729--0.820), PolyPhen-2 0.683 (0.613--0.750) and SIFT 0.656 (0.624--0.688). We verify that the deployed context window is 8193 bases and the calibration context is 8192 bases, a one-base difference confirmed for all 500 variants. We show that the deployed threshold was calibrated on GRCh37 while the service answers for GRCh38, that the two assemblies differ by a constant 1{,}847{,}983 bases at BRCA1, and that the browser forwards GRCh38 coordinates regardless. Across 55 generated request cases, the two independent validation layers agree on 49. We report eight defects with reproduction steps. Evo2Serve is a research prototype; its labels are computational scores, not clinical interpretations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Interpreting a genomic variant requires more than a score. The ACMG/AMP framework classifies sequence variants from evidence of several kinds, and it treats computational prediction as supporting evidence only~\cite{richards2015acmg}. The framework states that such predictions should not serve as the sole basis for a clinical assertion, and it warns that predictors vary widely in accuracy across genes. A variant classifier that ignores this guidance is a research tool, whatever its interface looks like.

Sequence models have changed what a computational predictor can offer. Evo 2 models DNA at single-nucleotide resolution with a context of up to one million base pairs, and the model was pretrained on the OpenGenome2 corpus without human variation or functional genomics data~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. On the BRCA1 saturation genome editing benchmark, the published zero-shot result for the 7B checkpoint is an AUROC of 0.9037 over 3{,}893 single-nucleotide variants~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. Such a result justifies attention. It does not by itself justify a particular label attached to a particular coordinate entered by a particular person.

Between the two sits a software layer. A user selects an assembly, picks a gene, reads a sequence, and types a coordinate and an alternate base. The application resolves the coordinate against a reference genome, retrieves a context window, scores a reference sequence and an edited sequence, subtracts the two scores, compares the difference with a threshold, and renders a label and a confidence bar. Each of these steps can fail, and the failures are invisible: a coordinate in the wrong assembly produces a confident answer about a different base.

This paper asks what a deployed genomic language model service must demonstrate before its output can be read as evidence. We take Evo2Serve as the object of study and answer through measurement rather than assertion. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A formal specification of the deployed scoring contract, covering window construction, the four supported edit operations, the score difference, the threshold rule and the confidence transform, written to be checkable against the source.
\item A reproducible characterization of the calibration data. We recompute the class composition of the source assay from the supplementary workbook and recover the published proportions exactly, then show that the 500-row slice the deployed threshold was fitted on is class-enriched and position-localized relative to the assay it is drawn from.
\item A reference envelope for that slice. We compute the first reproducible AUROC values for CADD, PolyPhen-2, SIFT, aGVD, phyloP and gnomAD allele frequency on the identical rows, under the identical label definition and the identical threshold-selection procedure, with bootstrap intervals. These values fix what a deployed scorer must exceed on this data, and they are computed without a GPU.
\item Verification of the windowing and assembly contracts. We show that the deployed context window differs from the calibration context window by one 3' base for all 500 calibration variants, and that the deployed threshold was fitted on GRCh37 while the service answers for GRCh38, where the two assemblies differ by a constant 1{,}847{,}983 base pairs across the BRCA1 locus.
\item A conformance suite over 55 generated request cases, executed against both validation layers of the deployed service, together with eight defects that the suite reproduces and that a reader can re-execute.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper describes the system, defines the contract, reports the measurements, and states what the measurements do and do not license. Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work among variant effect predictors and deployed interpretation services. Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the implementation. Section~\ref{sec:methods} gives the contract and the evaluation design. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the measurements. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them. Section~\ref{sec:limits} states the threats to validity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Variant Effect Predictors}
Computational prediction of variant effect has a long history. SIFT scores conservation from sequence neighbourhoods~\cite{ng2001sift}, PolyPhen-2 predicts damaging missense substitutions from multiple sequence alignments~\cite{adzhubei2010polyphen2}, and CADD aggregates many annotations into a single deleteriousness score~\cite{rentzsch2019cadd,schubach2024cadd}. The Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor assembles many of these into a standard annotation pipeline~\cite{mclaren2016vep,hunt2022vep}. AlphaMissense applies a protein language model across the whole proteome~\cite{cheng2023alphamissense}, and ESM-2 and its relatives extend language modelling to protein structure~\cite{lin2023esm2}. The ACMG/AMP framework assigns computational evidence a supporting weight at best, and the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group has since set out how a continuous computational score should be converted into PP3 and BP4 evidence through local calibration~\cite{pejaver2022svi,tavtigian2018acmg,tavtigian2020points}.

\subsection{Genomic Language Models}
Models that operate on nucleotide sequences directly include Nucleotide Transformer~\cite{dallatorre2024nt}, Enformer~\cite{avsec2021enformer}, Borzoi~\cite{linder2025borzoi}, DNABERT-2~\cite{zhou2024dnabert2}, Evo~\cite{nguyen2024evo} and Evo 2~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. Evo 2 differs in scale and context. It uses the StripedHyena 2 architecture, a convolutional multi-hybrid of input-dependent convolution operators and attention~\cite{brixi2025stripedhyena2}, and its 7B and 40B checkpoints were trained with a context extended to one million tokens. The 40B checkpoint consumed 9.3 trillion training tokens drawn from a corpus of more than 8.8 trillion nucleotides~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. The same paper reports that Evo 2 reaches an AUROC of 0.9037 on the BRCA1 saturation benchmark at the 7B scale, against 0.8763 for CADD and 0.8628 for GPN-MSA, and that it does not beat a supervised ridge classifier fitted on its own embeddings, which reaches 0.95 on a held-out split~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. An alignment-based model, GPN-MSA, reaches 0.8628 zero-shot without a task-specific fit~\cite{benegas2025gpnmsa}. Sequence-only models have also been shown to lag behind alignment-based ones on human variant effect in general~\cite{brixi2026evo2}.

Several qualifications apply to this literature. Genomic language models trained on sequence alone can capture phylogenetic bias rather than functional constraint, and correcting for that bias improves their variant effect predictions~\cite{pugh2025likelihood}. A classical phylogenetic method has been reported to outperform large protein language models on variant effect prediction~\cite{prillo2024siterm}. Variant effect predictors show ancestry-dependent behaviour~\cite{pathak2024ancestry}, and the same variant can receive different predicted effects in different genetic backgrounds~\cite{rastogi2025critical}. Recent benchmarks show that sequence models perform inadequately on some variant effect tasks, particularly for common variants~\cite{hosseini2025motifdiff}, and that computational annotations derived from the same inputs are not independent evidence~\cite{richards2015acmg}.

\subsection{Deployed Interpretation Services}
Several browser tools expose variant interpretation. VarSome aggregates many sources behind one query interface~\cite{vandenbroucke2019varsome}, and MobiDetails presents curated variant evidence online~\cite{baux2021mobidetails}. The Evo 2 authors publish Evo Designer and a mechanism-interpretation visualiser, and EVEE serves variant effect predictions from genomic foundation model embeddings through an interactive application~\cite{pearce2026evee,brandes2023esm1b}. Evo2Serve differs in that it is not an annotation aggregator: it exposes one model's sequence score for an arbitrary user-entered coordinate, and it calibrates that score into a binary label.

That distinction sets the question this paper addresses. Aggregation tools inherit the curation quality of the sources they display. Evo2Serve derives its own label from its own threshold, so the label stands or falls with the threshold, the window and the coordinate handling. Those three are the subject of Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\textit{Front end.} The client requests the human assembly list from UCSC and lets the user choose one; the default is hg38. It searches the NCBI gene table and shows at most ten results. Gene details come from an NCBI E-summary call. UCSC supplies the selected interval, and the interface limits the displayed sequence to 10{,}000 bases. ClinVar records are retrieved through E-utilities with a limit of twenty records, using the \texttt{chrpos37} field when the assembly is hg19 and the \texttt{chrpos38} field otherwise. The gene view combines sequence, gene details, a variant entry panel and a known-variant table. Clicking a displayed base fills the variant position and the reference base.

\textit{Back end.} The Modal image is based on CUDA 12.6.3 and installs PyTorch 2.7.1 and FlashAttention 2.8.0.post2 before the Evo 2 package. The model is loaded when a container starts, and a Hugging Face cache volume is mounted at the cache path. The FastAPI endpoint accepts a POST request, validates the payload with Pydantic, verifies the JWT, retrieves the context window from UCSC, scores both sequences and returns the difference, the label, the threshold and the confidence value. The Next.js route validates the same payload with Zod and checks the session cookie before forwarding it. Modal is configured for at most three containers with a 120-second scale-down window and two retries. These are configuration values, not measured throughput.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The Scoring Contract}
We write the deployed contract in the notation the source uses. Let $p$ be the 1-based coordinate of the substitution, the insertion anchor or the first affected base of a deletion or delins. Let $n$ be the deletion length and $q$ the inserted string. The window centre $c$ is $p$ for a substitution or an insertion, and $p+\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor$ for a deletion or a delins. Let the configured window parameter be $W=8192$ and its half width be $h=\lfloor W/2\rfloor=4096$. The backend requests the half-open interval
\begin{equation}
s=\max(0,c-1-h), \qquad e=c-1+h+1
\label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
from UCSC. The start is clipped at zero. The end is not clipped locally, so the returned length depends on the chromosome end. At an interior coordinate the interval has length $W+1=8193$, because both half widths contain the central base. Let $w$ be the returned upper-case sequence and let $r=p-1-s$ be the zero-based offset of the edit. Figure~\ref{fig:edits} shows the four edit operations, and Fig.~\ref{fig:window} contrasts the deployed interval with the calibration interval.

Formally, with the alternate base $a$, the edited sequence is
\begin{align}
w^{\mathrm{SNV}} &= w_{0:r} + a + w_{r+1:}, \label{eq:snv}\\
w^{\mathrm{DEL}} &= w_{0:r} + w_{r+n:}, \label{eq:del}\\
w^{\mathrm{INS}} &= w_{0:r+1} + q + w_{r+1:}, \label{eq:ins}\\
w^{\mathrm{DELINS}} &= w_{0:r} + q + w_{r+n:}. \label{eq:delins}
\end{align}
The interface caps $n$ and $|q|$ at 500 and rejects bases outside the four nucleotides.

\subsection{Score Difference, Label and Confidence}
Let $S(\cdot)$ be the mean per-base log-likelihood returned by \texttt{model.score\_sequences}. The upstream implementation applies a log-softmax over the vocabulary, shifts the result by one position, gathers the log-probability of the observed next base and averages over the valid positions~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. The quantity is therefore in nats per base pair, and the reference and the edited sequence are averaged over different numbers of positions whenever an edit changes the sequence length. The score difference is
\begin{equation}
\Delta = S(w^{\mathrm{var}}) - S(w^{\mathrm{ref}}),
\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
and a negative $\Delta$ indicates that the model finds the edited sequence less likely. The endpoint scores the reference and the edited sequence on every request and performs no cross-request caching.

The calibration routine selects its operating point by maximising Youden's index on the receiver operating characteristic curve of $-\Delta$~\cite{hanley1982roc,youden1950index}:
\begin{equation}
J(t)=\mathrm{TPR}(t)-\mathrm{FPR}(t), \qquad t^{\star}=\arg\max_t J(t),
\label{eq:youden}
\end{equation}
with $y=1$ for loss of function and $y=0$ for the functional and intermediate classes. The deployed endpoint instead uses the constant $\tau=-0.0009178519$ and applies
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{call}=\begin{cases}
\text{Likely pathogenic}, & \Delta < \tau,\\
\text{Likely benign}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:call}
\end{equation}
and the confidence transform
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{conf}=\min\!\left(1,\frac{|\Delta-\tau|}{\sigma_{\mathrm{class}}}\right),
\label{eq:conf}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_{\mathrm{LOF}}=0.0015140239$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{FUNC}}=0.0009016589$. Figure~\ref{fig:conf} shows the transform. The deletion, insertion and delins paths reuse the substitution constants and set a provisional flag on the response. The value is a capped distance from a threshold. It is not a probability and it has not been shown to be calibrated.

\subsection{Evaluation Design}
We evaluate the service along five axes, and we separate what we measured from what we could not measure.

\emph{Calibration data.} We read the supplementary workbook that ships with the repository and recompute its class composition, then characterise the 500-row slice that the deployed threshold was fitted on. We verify every reference base against the GRCh37 chr17 FASTA that ships with the same directory, and we verify that FASTA against the live UCSC hg19 service.

\emph{Reference envelope.} The same workbook carries pre-computed annotations for four established predictors, a conservation score and a population frequency. We evaluate them on the identical 500 rows, with the identical label definition and the identical threshold selection as the calibration routine, and we report stratified bootstrap confidence intervals over 2{,}000 resamples with a fixed seed.

\emph{Window and assembly contract.} We recompute both window rules over the 500 calibration positions using the shipped FASTA, and we align the GRCh37 and GRCh38 sequences across the BRCA1 locus using conserved anchors, then verify the derived offset against the NCBI Gene model and against the per-assembly locations in live ClinVar records.

\emph{Validation conformance.} We generate a matrix of 55 request cases that covers every discriminating field of the request schema and the boundary of every numeric and length constraint. We execute the matrix against both validation layers of the service, using the real Pydantic model imported from the backend module and the real Zod schema extracted from the route handler.

\emph{Scoring contract.} We import the deployed window and scoring functions and exercise them with a stand-in scorer, so that the arithmetic of the contract can be checked without a graphics processor. The stand-in is a third-order Markov background model estimated from the shipped FASTA, scored with the same mean reduction the model uses. The stand-in is not Evo 2. The contract behaviour it exercises does not depend on which model supplies the two log-likelihoods.

The analysis scripts, the intermediate results and the figure data are part of the repository under \texttt{docs/analysis}, and every number in Section~\ref{sec:results} is produced by one of them. We refer to them by the letters used in Table~\ref{tab:defects}: \textit{a} characterises the calibration data, \textit{b} computes the reference envelope, \textit{c} verifies the assembly provenance, \textit{d} and \textit{e} execute the two validation layers against the request matrix and against live ClinVar records, \textit{f} compares the two layers, and \textit{g} exercises the scoring contract.

\subsection{The Window Contract}
\label{sec:res-window}

The calibration routine and the deployed endpoint do not build the same context window. The routine takes the interval $[p-4096,\,p+4096)$ around the zero-based position, which is 8192 bases and places the variant at offset 4096. The endpoint takes $[p-1-4096,\,p+4096)$, which is 8193 bases and also places the variant at offset 4096. The deployed window is therefore the calibration window with one additional base at its 3' end.

We verified this for every one of the 500 calibration positions against the shipped GRCh37 FASTA: in all 500 cases the endpoint window is byte-identical to the calibration window except for the final base, and in 500 cases the two windows share their first 8192 bases. No variant falls at a chromosome boundary, so no clipping occurs.

The difference is small but not nil, and it is systematic. The score difference $\Delta$ is a difference of two averages over 8193 positions in the deployed path and over 8192 positions in the calibration path. A change in the window length shifts both the content and the number of terms over which the mean is taken, so the deployed distribution of $\Delta$ is not the distribution the threshold was fitted on. The magnitude of the shift is not measurable without re-running the model, which is why Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists it as a threat rather than a result.

\subsection{Assembly Provenance}
\label{sec:res-assembly}

The calibration data are in GRCh37. The service answers for GRCh38 by default, and the application offers every human assembly that UCSC publishes. Nothing in the request path converts between assemblies, and the request carries no reference allele against which the fetched sequence could be checked.

We measured what that costs. Aligning the GRCh37 and GRCh38 sequences across the BRCA1 locus using 681 conserved anchors gives a constant offset of 1{,}847{,}983 base pairs; all 680 consecutive anchor pairs are free of indels, so the offset does not drift across the gene. The offset agrees with the NCBI Gene model for BRCA1 and with the per-assembly locations that ClinVar reports for all 290 live BRCA1 records that carry both a GRCh37 and a GRCh38 position, where the difference is again exactly 1{,}847{,}983 in every case.

The consequences follow directly. Table~\ref{tab:assembly} shows them. A user who enters a GRCh37 coordinate while the GRCh38 assembly is selected scores a sequence window that shares no base with the intended one, and the reference base at that coordinate is wrong for all 500 calibration variants. A user who enters a correct GRCh38 coordinate scores the intended sequence, because BRCA1 is identical between the two assemblies across the whole 8{,}192-base window. The offset is therefore a coordinate-naming problem rather than a sequence problem for this gene, which is the more dangerous of the two: the service returns a confident answer either way.

\subsection{Behaviour of the Scoring Contract}
\label{sec:res-contract}

We exercised the deployed window and scoring functions with a stand-in scorer, to check the arithmetic of the contract rather than the behaviour of the model. A substitution at a BRCA1 position produced a score difference of $-1.03\times10^{-4}$, which is the same order of magnitude as the deployed threshold of $-9.18\times10^{-4}$. A deletion of three bases produced scored sequences of 8193 and 8190 bases, and an insertion of three bases produced 8193 and 8196 bases.

A substitution that leaves the base unchanged is accepted by the service and produces a score difference of exactly zero, because the edited sequence is then byte-identical to the reference sequence. A zero difference lies above the threshold, so the service returns the benign label, and the confidence transform returns
\begin{equation}
\min\!\left(1,\frac{|0-\tau|}{\sigma_{\mathrm{FUNC}}}\right)=1.000000.
\label{eq:noop}
\end{equation}
The service therefore returns its maximum confidence for a request that changes nothing. The defect is independent of the model: it follows from the arithmetic of Eq.~\eqref{eq:conf}, and it would be repaired either by comparing the submitted allele with the fetched reference, which the request format does not carry, or by treating a zero difference as an invalid request.

Each request also performs two forward passes, one for the reference window and one for the edited window. The offline calibration routine instead de-duplicates reference windows, scoring 168 references for 500 variants, which is 33.2\% fewer passes than the naive count. The deployed path re-scores the same reference window on every request.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Measurements License}
The measurements in Section~\ref{sec:results} support four claims and no more.

First, the calibration slice is a narrow and unrepresentative sample. It is enriched 7.79-fold for the positive class relative to uniform sampling of the assay, it covers 10.69\% of the assayed interval, and its class mix changes by a factor of 2.3 between its first and second halves. A threshold fitted on it describes the score distribution of that slice, not of the assay and not of the genome.

Second, the slice resolves predictor differences poorly. The bootstrap intervals in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} overlap between neighbouring predictors, and the same annotation moves by 0.09 AUROC between the slice and the full assay. Any comparison of a deployed scorer with the published Evo 2 figures must be made on the full set, with the slice restriction stated.

Third, the service implements a well-defined contract that is not the contract its threshold was fitted under. The window differs by one base, the assembly differs by 1{,}847{,}983 bases, the coordinate path ignores the selected assembly, and the confidence transform returns its maximum for a null edit. None of these is visible from the interface.

Fourth, the deployed service is not a clinical classifier, and the literature is explicit on this point. ACMG/AMP assigns computational evidence a supporting weight and warns against using it as the sole basis for an assertion~\cite{richards2015acmg}; the BRCA1 saturation assay makes the same point for conservation-based predictors~\cite{findlay2018brca1}; and the ClinGen calibration standard requires a local positive predictive value before a computational score can support a classification~\cite{pejaver2022svi}. The labels the service emits, ``Likely pathogenic'' and ``Likely benign'', use the vocabulary of a clinical classification without the evidentiary structure behind it. The gene-specific specifications for BRCA1 and BRCA2 show what that structure requires: functional-domain boundaries, allele-frequency thresholds and splice predictors~\cite{clinGenBrcaVcep2025}.

\subsection{What a Deployment Should Do Instead}
The defects in Table~\ref{tab:defects} are individually easy to repair, and the repairs are specific.

The request format should carry the reference allele, and the endpoint should compare it with the fetched reference and reject a mismatch. This single check catches the wrong-assembly case, the null-edit case and the off-by-one coordinate case at once, because all three produce a reference base that differs from the one the user intended. The endpoint should also validate the assembly against the list UCSC publishes, and should refuse values containing a semicolon.

The two validation layers should be generated from one schema rather than written twice, so that the route handler and the endpoint cannot disagree.

The threshold should be recalibrated on the full assay, on a held-out split, and under the deployed window rather than the calibration window. The reported operating point should include an interval, and the confidence value should be replaced by a calibrated quantity. The method in~\cite{pejaver2022svi} provides a route to the latter.

The context should be cached across requests. The window is a function of the coordinate, and the offline routine already demonstrates the de-duplication: 168 reference windows serve 500 variants.

\subsection{Positioning}
Evo2Serve sits with the deployed interpretation services rather than with the annotation aggregators. VarSome and MobiDetails show curated evidence and let the reader weigh it~\cite{vandenbroucke2019varsome,baux2021mobidetails}; Evo Designer and EVEE expose a model's own view of a sequence~\cite{pearce2026evee}. Evo2Serve does the same and adds a calibration step that a reader cannot inspect, which is the distinction that matters for interpretation.

Two observations from the published literature bear on what the service should claim. Evo 2 outperforms earlier sequence-only models on this benchmark by a wide margin, with an AUROC of 0.9037 against 0.5496 for the Nucleotide Transformer, yet the improvement over a supervised classifier fitted on its own embeddings is modest, and the gain is concentrated in non-coding variants~\cite{brixi2026evo2}. A service that turns a score into a binary label discards the stratification that makes the score informative, because a single threshold must serve coding and non-coding variants with very different score distributions. The service should return the score and a calibrated probability alongside any label, and should abstain where the calibration does not apply.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

We did not execute the model. The evaluation environment has no graphics processor, so no Evo 2 score was generated and no AUROC is reported for the deployed service. Where we state that the service returns a confident answer for a null edit, we mean that the behaviour follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:conf} and is independent of the model. Where we state that the deployed window differs from the calibration window, we mean that the sequences differ, which we verified; we do not state how much the score difference moves.

The reference envelope rests on annotations pre-computed by other authors and stored in the source workbook. We did not recompute them, so the values in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} measure those annotations as published on these rows, not the predictors themselves.

The evaluation covers one gene, one assay and one slice. The assembly analysis covers BRCA1, where the two assemblies are identical across the scoring window. A gene with indels between GRCh37 and GRCh38 would show a different window identity, and the offset would not be constant.

The validation matrix is generated rather than exhaustive. It covers the discriminating fields and the documented boundaries, but it does not cover every combination of the request schema, and it does not exercise the HTTP layer, the session handling or the Modal deployment configuration.

The live ClinVar results depend on the state of the database on the day of the run, and the record set is capped at 300 by the query. The title-parsing results in particular depend on how many records of a given type the query returns.

Finally, the paper reports no latency or throughput measurement. The configuration allows three containers with a 120-second scale-down window and two retries, and the contract requires two forward passes over 8193 bases per request. What that costs in wall-clock time is a property of the deployment and of the model, and we did not measure it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We described the scoring contract of a deployed genomic language model service and measured it. The calibration data reproduce the published composition of the source assay, and the 500-row slice used for the deployed threshold is shown to be class-enriched and position-localized. We established the first reproducible reference envelope for that slice, with bootstrap intervals, and the published Evo 2 BRCA1 results give the comparison point. We verified that the deployed context window and the calibration window differ by one base, that the deployed threshold was fitted on GRCh37 while the service answers for GRCh38, and that the browser forwards GRCh38 coordinates whatever assembly is selected. Across 55 generated request cases the two validation layers disagree on 6 and both accept 7 malformed assemblies or chromosomes. Eight defects are reproducible from the scripts in the repository.

The conclusion for practice is narrow. Evo2Serve is a usable interface for reading a model's opinion about a sequence, and it is not yet a calibrated variant classifier. The threshold it applies has no run artifact, the coordinate it accepts is unchecked, and the confidence it shows is a distance from a constant. Each of these has a specific repair, and the first of them, carrying the reference allele in the request, closes most of the rest.
